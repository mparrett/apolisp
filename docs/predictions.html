The claim that exists to catch a flattering result

Three times, before doing the work, apolisp wrote down numbered predictions of what the work would do — and scored them afterwards against measurement. Thirteen claims. Five held cleanly. The two that paid for the whole habit were the ones written specifically to make a good-looking answer impossible to accept.

The habit

Two lines in a commit message, and what they are for

The rule in BUILD.md is small enough to quote whole:

Before running a benchmark, write down what you expect and why. Afterwards, record whether it was refuted. … Two lines in a commit message is enough. This is a habit, not a document.

It exists because of a specific hole. ADR-021 removed the gate on optimisation work — there is no committee to convince, no performance budget to defend, and a change can go in because it looked worth trying. That freedom is the point of a project with one user, and it has an obvious failure mode: every change ships with a story about why it worked, assembled after the numbers were in. Pre-registration is what turns the freedom into knowledge rather than folklore.

The prior art was already doing it. ../wallisp benchmarks eight engine architectures with pre-registered predictions and a falsification log per experiment, and its most useful finding is one of its own hypotheses being falsified: refcounting came out the slowest collector tested, ~1.1–1.25× worse than mark-sweep, because it pays inc/dec eagerly on every reference while a lazy mark-sweep barely fires. That result argues against ADR-003 here, which is exactly the kind of thing a falsification log is for and a success story never produces.

Three times the habit outgrew two lines in a commit message and became a note written before the work existed. Those three are what this page scores. The mutation passes use the same discipline against a different subject — a declared verdict per mutant, written before the run — and that story is told elsewhere.

2026-07-28the editor

A program predicted before it was written

No editor existed. Three falsifiable claims about where a full-screen text editor would hit a wall. The three programs before it were written without a pre-registration, and in each case the interesting result had to be reconstructed afterwards from what had surprised whoever was typing.

2026-07-29str-scalar-slice

A primitive predicted before it existed

Five claims about a scalar-indexed substring, filed against Q34 while the operation was still an open question. Became ADR-052.

2026-07-30vec-slice

A shape chosen against a rival, in advance

Five claims, and the entry states the bet it is making: a general slice composed at the call site, against splice, a single-allocation primitive shaped for exactly two callers. Became ADR-053.

The device

Write the claim that makes the flattering reading impossible

Both slice pre-registrations contain a claim whose stated purpose is to spoil a result the author would otherwise want to believe. In the first, it is claim 2, and the note gives its reason in the entry itself: “This claim exists because the failure mode of claim 1 is a flattering one.”

Claim 1 was that per-keystroke cost would go near-flat — about 4 µs, barely rising across the range the editor cares about. Claim 2 said: if that is what the small numbers show, push until the linear term appears, because the operation is a scan and a curve that looks flat at 1,600 characters is a curve measured too close in.

Claim 1~4 µs floor, a 1.3–2× rise from 100 to 1,600 characters, 1,500–2,500× faster than the old idiom.
Measured2,134× at 1,600 characters — inside the band. Everything about why was wrong: the real floor is ~0.5 µs, so the linear term is visible almost immediately and the rise is 4.9×. Four times better than predicted, on a curve four times worse.
Claim 2The scan shows by 25,000 characters, ≥20 µs. Measured 43.58 µs, and the 100,000-character point settles the shape: 4× the characters costs 3.80×.

Here is the sentence that justifies the entire practice, from the scoring:

The small-string numbers on their own look flat, and had I stopped at 1,600 I would have written “flat” into an ADR that says linear.

Nothing else in the process would have caught that. The benchmark was correct, the speedup was real, the decision was right, and the sentence about to be written into the permanent record was false. What stopped it was a note, written a day earlier, saying in advance that this specific good news should not be accepted at this specific size.

The next round wrote the same kind of claim on purpose, and it was again the one that mattered. vec-slice predicted RET at 1,000 lines would drop about 70× — and then predicted, separately, that the growth rate would fall to 3.5–4.5× per 4× buffer, “because claim 1 could be satisfied by a large constant-factor win that leaves the exponent intact, and that is the flattering reading.”

Claim 17.244 ms → ~0.10 ms, about 70×.
Measured0.0288 ms — 252×. Held, and underestimated by 3.5×: four copies of a 1,000-element vector were costed as if a copy were expensive, and a memcpy of 1,000 pointers is not.
Claim 23.5–4.5× per 4× buffer. Measured 2.71×, 3.47×, 3.80× — converging on 4.0 from below as the fixed overhead washes out. The exponent really did go.

A 252× speedup is the kind of number that ends an investigation. The reason it did not is that the thing which would have made it hollow — a constant-factor win dressed as an algorithmic one — had been named as a distinct, checkable claim before anyone knew what the numbers would be.

The transferable part

For every prediction of the form “X gets faster”, write the second claim that distinguishes the fix worked from the fix looks like it worked. It is almost always about shape rather than magnitude: does the exponent move, does the curve stay flat when pushed an order of magnitude further, does the cost still track the input it should.

The threshold

Name the result that would say the decision was wrong, not the number

Both slice entries carry a section headed “what would refute the entry rather than a number”, and it is a different instrument from the claims above. A miscalibrated prediction is ordinary. What the threshold names is the measurement that would say the choice was wrong — that the ADR picked the wrong shape and should be superseded rather than merely annotated.

EntryThe result that would have said “wrong shape”Measured
ADR-052Per-keystroke cost above ~50 µs at 1,600 characters — the linear scan is not cheap enough, and a cached scalar→byte offset was the better primitive.4.54 µs
ADR-053RET above 1 ms at 1,000 lines — four allocations are the cost after all, and splice was the right shape.0.0288 ms
ADR-053Secondary: take/drop show no improvement, which empties the general-case argument that is the entire reason vec-slice was chosen over splice.1,706×

Neither threshold was met, and the second was missed by three orders of magnitude. That is a boring outcome and it is worth exactly what it cost, because of what the threshold prevents rather than what it found: once a number like 0.0288 ms is on the table, every reading of it is favourable. The pre-registration fixes in advance which readings are allowed to count as vindication of the choice, so a decision that survives can be said to have survived something.

The distinction between the two instruments matters. str-scalar-slice got three of five predictions wrong and its threshold was never approached — so the honest summary is the decision was right and most of the reasoning about it was miscalibrated, which is a sentence that can only be written because the two were separated before the run.

The calibration

What is wrong is never the direction

Thirteen claims across three rounds, and not one of them pointed the wrong way. Everything that failed, failed on magnitude or on mechanism — and the mechanism failures are the ones worth reading, because a wrong mechanism survives being right about the number.

The editor pre-registration is the extreme case. Its central claim was that per-keystroke cost would be governed by assoc copying the whole lines vector, N refcount bumps per keystroke, comfortable to about 50,000 lines. The editor got built the same day.

PredictedTyping is comfortable to ~50,000 lines, because the per-keystroke cost is the spine copy and the spine copy is cheap enough.
MeasuredComfortable, and not for that reason. Buffer size is nearly free — 8× the lines costs 1.12×. Cost tracks line length, because the cursor is a character column, str-slice takes bytes, and the character-safe surface is built from prelude conj loops. An edit is quadratic in the column, and at 1,600 characters that is 12.6 ms against the native path’s 4 µs.

Right answer, wrong axis. And the third claim in that file was “it will be misdiagnosed” — recorded because the symptom is a whole-file operation and the cause is a per-element clone, so the expected first explanation would be “the VM is slow”. That claim held, and it was held by the document making it. The misdiagnosis on record is its own.

The same pattern, smaller, in round two: the predicted speedup ratio at 1,600 characters landed inside the band while both terms of the ratio were wrong — the floor by 8× and the slope by about 3×. Being right about a ratio through two compensating errors is not a calibrated prediction; it is a coincidence that looks like one, and it is only visible because the note recorded the terms and not just the ratio.

The habit this argues for

Predict the terms, not the summary statistic. A predicted speedup can be confirmed by two errors that cancel; a predicted floor and a predicted slope cannot both be confirmed by accident. The claims here that produced real information are, without exception, the ones stated as a shape or a mechanism rather than as a single number.

The repeat offender

Twice now, “the code gets smaller” has lost to prose

The dullest claim in each entry is the line-count one. The project has a hard budget — 7,500 lines — so every change predicts its own cost, and this is the prediction that has been wrong the most consistently.

ADR-052The editor core loses 2–4 lines; the change costs 25–35 core lines.
ActualThe core lost one line: two definitions went away and a six-line comment explaining why took their place. The change cost 57, and the 20-line overrun is entirely comment.
ADR-053The prelude gets shorter — take and drop lose their loops.
ActualThe prelude got longer, 195 → 201, because the comment explaining where clamping now lives is bigger than the two loops it replaced. The core total landed at 6,637, inside its predicted band.

ADR-030 counts comments deliberately, so this is a real cost and not a bookkeeping quibble. The rule the second round wrote down, having lost the same way twice:

On a change whose point is a subtle invariant, the explanation is the bulk of the diff, and estimating the code is not estimating the change.

It generalises past line counts. Both of these changes were small, surgical, and correct, and both were mostly writing — because what a subtle change costs is not the instructions it adds but the sentence a future reader needs in order not to undo it.

The unpredicted

Each round’s largest finding was not on its list

This is the uncomfortable one. In all three rounds the most valuable thing learned was absent from the pre-registration entirely.

  • Round one predicted a buffer-size cliff and found a line-length one: str-slice is native and byte-indexed, the character-aware surface is correct and quadratic, and they are not the same operation. ADR-049 had made the safe path the slow path, and no program had noticed because none had done character surgery in a loop. A 2,905× gap, absent from the file that predicted the cliff.
  • Round two made typing 20× faster and RET 1.8×, so the gap between the two widened from ~60× to ~660×. Fixing the cheap operation is what made the expensive one conspicuous. Filed as Q36, which is round three.
  • Round three found that typing had become linear in the buffer — 3.72× per 4× lines — because insert-str calls assoc, a native O(n) copy. That was always true. It was invisible while the quadratic string path sat on top of it.

Read in order, those are not three separate surprises. They are one mechanism, three times:

Round 1The first editor benchmark could not see past the scalar conj loops.
Round 2ADR-052 removed them, typing got 20× faster, and RET became the visible cost.
Round 3ADR-053 made RET 252× faster, and assoc became the visible cost.

A benchmark on a system with one dominant quadratic measures the quadratic, and every other cost in the program is unmeasured until it is gone.

Which puts a bound on what any of these pre-registrations could have contained. Round one could not have predicted the assoc cost; nothing could measure it yet. The pre-registration is not weaker for having missed it, and the honest reading is not “the predictions keep missing the big thing” but “the big thing is by construction the one currently hidden, and a fix is the instrument that exposes it”.

The editor entry anticipated this in its scoring instructions, which is the best thing in it:

The prediction is wrong as a whole if the editor turns out to be blocked by something absent from this file — in which case the useful finding is not the blocker but the fact that reading the source and writing three programs did not surface it.

It was so blocked, twice: concat returns a list that assoc refuses, and the language has no type predicates. Neither appears anywhere in the file. Both were found in the first hour of writing the program.

What it bought

An optimisation refuted before anyone spent a day on it

The editor pre-registration proposed a line-level gap buffer to dodge the per-keystroke assoc. It is a reasonable idea, it is what an editor is supposed to want, and it would have taken a day.

The benchmark killed it in an afternoon. Buffer size is already free; the cost is inside a single line. If anything here wants a gap buffer it is the line, not the vector of lines — and a scalar-indexed slice removes the reason to want one at all.

The clearest thing the habit has paid for

A plausible optimisation, written down as a proposal before the measurement existed, and refuted by the measurement rather than by taste. Had the gap buffer been built first it would have been built, measured, found to help slightly, and kept — because a change that helps a little is nearly impossible to argue out of a codebase once it is in.

The same file’s structural prediction — that testability, not capability would shape the program, and that written as anything other than a pure state × key → state core the editor could not be tested at all — held, with one correction it is worth having made. “An editor cannot be pinned by the corpus” turned out to be true only of the 62-line shell: the core replays nineteen chords through reduce dispatch with no terminal anywhere and has an ordinary .out golden. That one is its own story.

The method

Twice, the prediction found the bug in the instrument rather than the code

A pre-registration is a check on the work. It turns out to be a better check on the apparatus, and both times it caught one, the mechanism was the same: the apparatus produced a result indistinguishable from a real one, and the wrong reading was the flattering one.

The mutant that never applied

Milestone 9 seeded twelve mutants into the session and the reader, each with a declared verdict written first. Two of them were substituted with a perl pattern containing an escape that the shell passed through unchanged, so the regex looked for two backslashes where the file has one. Neither substitution fired. Both runs reported a green suite — which is precisely what a surviving mutant reports.

DeclaredMutant 12 — the prompt drops the suppressed chain — will survive, and here is the specific reason.
ObservedSurvived. Checking the stated reason meant opening the file, and the file was unmodified.

Nothing distinguishes “the mutation survived” from “the mutation never happened” from outside the run, and one of those is a finding while the other is a wasted run being recorded as a finding. What caught it was not the harness. It was that the prediction had a reason attached, and verifying a reason means looking at the subject.

The mutant that applied and never reached the binary

The soak asserts that two programs run under valgrind lose no memory, and neither program can make that assertion fail — so the check itself was validated by mutation. Pre-registered: inject a definite leak into a hot path, expect it caught, and note that the interesting outcome would be a survivor.

mutant 1  std::mem::forget(Box::new([0u8; 64])) in conj
          churn.xs: 30 conj x 5,000 iters = 150,000 leaks

valgrind  definitely lost: 0 bytes in 0 blocks   exit 0
heap      1,482,053 allocs, 1,482,052 frees
                       ↑ these match, so nothing was ever allocated

The boxes were never allocated. A Box that is allocated, never read and never freed is unobservable, and LLVM is entitled to delete it. Wrapped in std::hint::black_box, the same mutant is caught immediately: 9,599,936 bytes in 149,999 blocks, exit 9.

This is a second way to get the same wrong answer, and the rule the previous case produced does not catch it. grep confirmed the mutation was in the source. The build recompiled. The mutation was real in src/lib.rs and absent from the machine code.

Read the counters, not the verdict

total heap usage is what distinguishes “nothing leaked” from “nothing happened”, and every instrument has an equivalent — an instruction count, a transcript, a timing. A verdict alone cannot tell the two apart. Anything mutated under --release is exposed, the vulnerable shape is a mutant that adds work with no observable effect, and the soak runs release because release is the profile it exists to test.

Both rules now live in BUILD.md next to the pre-registration rule, which is the right place for them: they are not facts about valgrind or about perl. They are the two ways a written-down expectation can be satisfied by a run that did not happen.

Nine of thirteen landed on the right side of the answer and four of those did it for a reason that turned out to be false. That ratio is the argument for the practice rather than against it: a habit that only confirmed things would tell you nothing, and one that was usually wrong about the direction would not be worth the five minutes. What this one reliably produces is a correct expectation attached to an incorrect explanation — and the explanation is the part that gets reused, in the next benchmark and in the next ADR.

Both entries that named a threshold still stand, and neither threshold was approached. It is possible to say that only because the conditions were written down while the answer was still unknown.
